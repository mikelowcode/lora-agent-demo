The Case for Local-First Software

Most software built today assumes a server somewhere is the source of
truth. Your notes, your messages, your files — they live on someone else's
machine, and your device is just a window onto them. That model has real
advantages: seamless sync across devices, effortless sharing, and no
responsibility for backups. It also has a cost that's easy to forget until
it's too late — the service can raise its price, change its terms, get
acquired, or simply shut down, and your data goes with it.

Local-first software takes a different starting position: your own device
holds the real copy of your data, and it works fully offline. Anything
networked — sync, search, collaboration — is an enhancement layered on top,
never a requirement for the software to function at all. If a cloud
service disappears tomorrow, a genuinely local-first tool keeps working
exactly as it did the day before.

For software that reads and writes personal context — notes, conversation
history, documents you've fed it — this matters more than usual. The value
of a personal assistant compounds over time: the more it knows about you,
the more useful it becomes. That's exactly the kind of data you don't want
tied to a subscription, a company's continued existence, or a network
connection. Keeping it local isn't a privacy feature bolted on afterward;
it's what makes the long-term value of the system possible at all.
